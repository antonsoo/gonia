# Which side of a chord contains the angle?

The new flute workshop reports constant 135 and 90 degree edge angles. Its
remaining teaching gap is the reason, particularly the tempting but incorrect
halving of the Doric cut's 90 degree arc to get 45 at the point in the hollow.

Keep the four-room architecture and existing state. A paired, equal-scale
circle view will put P on the cut's arc and Q on its complementary arc. The
existing point fraction moves both points; a local slider mirrors that same
shared setting. No second unshared construction state is needed. A and B stay
fixed. Highlight the arc opposite each selected point, and identify the circular
segment containing it. For Doric: P sees 270 degrees and makes 135; Q sees 90
and makes 45. Ionic: both see a semicircle and make 90. Flat faces have no
construction circle and should say so rather than inventing one.

Euclid III.31 supplies the ancient classification: angle IN the larger segment
is less than right; IN the smaller segment greater than right. Heiberg's Greek
and his 1883 Latin translation are on complete printed pages 240 and 241
(PDF 254 and 255). Retain both full pages, including the later statement about
the angle OF a segment, and explicitly distinguish its curved-boundary notion
from the straight-chord angles measured here. The Latin is a modern scholarly
translation, not an ancient Latin version.

The numeric degree calculation is modern notation. Show why P's two
isosceles triangles give base angles (180-t*phi)/2 and
(180-(1-t)*phi)/2, totaling 180-phi/2. Q is the opposite angle of a
cyclic quadrilateral, so their total is 180 (Euclid III.22). This explains the
numbers without assigning a 270-degree ordinary central angle to Euclid.
The 270 degree label describes an arc extent, not a claim about his angle
conventions. His III.31 classifies the angles; it does not prescribe a Doric
flute or these model dimensions.
